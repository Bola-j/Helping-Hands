# Helping Hands: Full Idea and Business Model

**Helping Hands is a platform that helps trusted community organizations coordinate requests for assistance with available goods, money, and organizational help.** Organizational help includes people’s time and skills, transport, facilities, and equipment.

The platform gives organizations one place to receive and review requests, coordinate offers, assign work, track fulfillment, and understand the effort and cost involved. The partner organization remains responsible for deciding who to help, managing donations, delivering support, and confirming outcomes.

The idea can cover many kinds of needs. The operating model focuses on three ways to meet them so the organization can manage the work and its overhead.

## The problem

A charity, religious institution, school, or community group may hear about people’s needs through calls, visits, referrals, and messages. At the same time, donors and volunteers offer goods, money, skills, vehicles, spaces, or equipment through separate channels.

When requests and offers are spread out, coordinators can struggle to answer basic questions:

- Which requests are still waiting for review?
- Which requests are urgent under the organization’s criteria?
- What goods are available, and what has already been reserved?
- Which volunteers or partners have the right skills and availability?
- Who is responsible for the next step?
- What did fulfillment require in staff time, volunteer hours, transport, storage, and direct costs?
- Was the request completed, partially completed, or left unresolved?

Helping Hands proposes a shared process and record for answering these questions. Whether that would save time or improve service is a hypothesis to validate with local organizations.

## Who it serves and how

**Beneficiaries** are individuals or families requesting help. They can submit a request directly or get assistance from a coordinator.

**Third-party requesters** are people who submit a request on someone else’s behalf, such as relatives, neighbors, teachers, or community members.

**Partner organizations**—such as charities, religious institutions, schools, clinics, or community groups—can refer people, submit requests for those they serve, and contribute or coordinate resources.

**Donors and contributors** offer goods, money through an appropriate organization, professional skills, time, transport, facilities, or equipment.

**Volunteers** accept defined tasks, such as packing, delivery, tutoring, translation, repairs, or event support.

**Coordinators** are the organization’s authorized users. They review and prioritize requests, approve or decline them, check offers, make matches, assign tasks, manage exceptions, and close requests.

## What kinds of needs it can coordinate

The platform can support different need categories, each with its own questions and handling steps. Possible categories include food and household essentials, clothing and furniture, education and tutoring, medical support, transport and accessibility, housing-related items, employment and skills support, emergency relief, and social or community support.

The category determines what information is needed. A transport request needs a destination and date. A tutoring request needs a subject, schedule, and suitable volunteer. A request for household goods needs item details and a way to receive or deliver them.

Not every organization will handle every category. Each partner sets its service scope and refers requests outside that scope to an appropriate provider where possible.

## The three forms of support

### Goods

Goods can be donated or purchased for approved requests. Examples include food, clothing, furniture, school supplies, tools, and equipment.

The organization records what was offered and what was actually received, checks suitability and condition, and tracks where the goods are stored. When an item is assigned to a request, its quantity is reserved. After delivery, the delivered quantity is recorded. This prevents goods from being promised to multiple requests and makes partial fulfillment visible.

Goods create operational overhead: receiving, checking, sorting, storage, inventory, packing, delivery, and handling unsuitable or damaged items. The partner organization decides which goods it can accept and how they should be stored and distributed.

### Money

Money can support requests where a suitable item or service is not available, or where the organization has an approved program for allocating funds. It could also help cover defined costs such as transport, repair, or essential purchases.

For every financial contribution, the organization records who is responsible for receiving and holding it, its authorized purpose, the approval and allocation, the amount spent, and any remaining balance. The platform can keep a coordination record, but the authorized organization must control the funds and its financial records.

Money creates overhead in donation administration, approvals, recordkeeping, allocation, and reporting. Fundraising and receipt rules depend on the organization and the way money is collected. Real money should therefore be handled through an appropriate partner organization that has checked the applicable requirements.

### Organizational help

Organizational help means a person or organization contributes capacity rather than a physical item or a direct financial contribution. It includes:

- **Time and skills:** tutoring, translation, repairs, professional guidance, event support, and similar services.
- **Transport:** rides, delivery vehicles, or collection services.
- **Facilities and equipment:** storage space, meeting rooms, tools, or distribution locations.

A contributor describes what they can offer, including location, schedule, capacity, duration, and any limits. A coordinator checks the offer, matches it to a suitable request, and creates a defined task or booking.

This form of support has its own overhead: recruiting and onboarding helpers, checking suitability, scheduling, supervision, communicating changes, arranging access, handling cancellations, and following up. The organization records hours committed and completed, transport or facility use, direct expenses, and coordinator time.

## How the full service works

A typical request moves through these steps:

1. **Submission:** A beneficiary, third party, or partner organization describes the need and selects a category. The form asks only for information relevant to that type of request.
2. **Review:** An authorized coordinator checks whether the request is understandable and within the organization’s service scope. They can approve it, decline it with a reason, or ask for more information.
3. **Prioritization:** The coordinator applies the organization’s agreed criteria, such as urgency, waiting time, and available resources. If priority changes, the reason is recorded.
4. **Matching:** The coordinator looks for suitable goods, money available through the partner, or organizational help. A request may need more than one type of support.
5. **Planning:** The coordinator identifies the people, goods, space, transport, schedule, and direct costs required. If only part of the need can be met, the remaining need stays visible.
6. **Assignment:** The coordinator assigns a volunteer or partner to a specific task. The assignment includes timing, location, expected outcome, limits, and a contact person.
7. **Fulfillment:** Goods are prepared and delivered; money is allocated and spent by the responsible organization; or organizational help is provided.
8. **Confirmation:** The helper records what happened. A coordinator reviews the record and closes the request, marks it partially completed, or sets the next action.
9. **Follow-up:** Where appropriate, the organization checks whether the support was useful and whether the need remains.

A general request status flow could be:

`Submitted → Under review → Approved / Declined / More information needed → Awaiting support → Matched → Assigned → In progress → Completed → Closed`

Additional statuses should cover partial completion, cancellation, or inability to complete, with a reason and next action.

## Business and operating model

Helping Hands is a **coordination service for organizations that connect community needs with available support**.

| Role | Contribution to the model |
|---|---|
| Beneficiaries | Describe needs and receive assistance |
| Charity or community organization | Operates the service, reviews requests, manages resources, and confirms fulfillment |
| Donors and businesses | Offer goods, approved financial support, transport, facilities, or equipment |
| Volunteers and professionals | Provide time, skills, or defined services |
| Helping Hands platform | Organizes requests, offers, decisions, assignments, inventory, and outcome records |
| Sponsor, grant provider, or subscribing organization | May fund setup and ongoing operation |

The partner organization is the operator and likely buyer or funding partner. Beneficiaries are the people served; donors and volunteers are contributors. These roles may overlap, but the model should not assume they are the same.

Possible ways to sustain the service include:

- a university or community-sponsored pilot;
- grants or corporate sponsorship;
- a future subscription or service agreement with organizations;
- setup, training, and support services for organizations adopting the platform.

These are options to validate. A partner may have limited budget, may require a sponsor, or may not want another software system. Interviews should establish who would adopt it, which tasks matter most, what training they need, and who could pay.

The service provider’s costs include software development, hosting, setup, training, and support. The partner’s aid-delivery costs include coordinator time, volunteer onboarding, storage, packing, transport, facility use, maintenance, and direct aid spending. Keeping these costs distinct helps determine whether the service is practical.

## Governance and accountability

These are the governance rules accepted for the model:

| Decision or activity | Rule |
|---|---|
| Approving or declining requests | Only authorized coordinators make the decision. Volunteers do not approve requests or their own assignments. |
| Setting priority | The organization defines consistent criteria; coordinators record reasons for priority changes. |
| Sharing information with donors | Donors see general needs and progress without beneficiaries’ identities or addresses. |
| Accessing beneficiary details | Coordinators and people assigned to a relevant task see only the details they need for that work. |
| Allocating goods | The system tracks available, reserved, and delivered quantities so goods are not allocated twice. |
| Confirming completion | The assigned helper records the outcome; a coordinator reviews and closes the request. |
| Handling volunteer cancellation | The task returns to the coordinator for reassignment. Reserved goods stay linked to the request until changed by a coordinator. |
| Reviewing complaints | A coordinator records a complaint; another authorized person reviews it where practical. |
| Responding to misuse | The organization can suspend access, investigate, and record the outcome. |
| Maintaining accountability | The system keeps a history of decisions, allocations, assignments, and status changes, including who made them and when. |

Additional rules for organizational help:

- Contributors state the limits of their offer, including schedule, service area, capacity, and conditions.
- An offer becomes available only after the organization confirms it.
- Coordinators check suitability and agree any needed supervision or access arrangements before assignment.
- Assignments explain the task, expected outcome, boundaries, and contact person.
- The organization records completion, cancellations, resource use, and follow-up effort.

Each partner organization remains responsible for its own eligibility criteria, volunteer conduct, safeguarding, complaint handling, and data-retention policies. Helping Hands provides a place to apply and record those rules; it does not set one policy for every organization.

## Supply chain and logistics

For goods, the chain is:

`Need identified → Offer received → Offer reviewed → Goods received and checked → Stored → Reserved → Packed → Collected or delivered → Confirmed`

For organizational help, the chain is:

`Need identified → Capacity offered → Offer checked → Matched → Scheduled → Assigned → Service or resource provided → Confirmed`

The coordinator plans what each request will require, including labor, time, storage, transport, access, and direct costs. If goods are damaged, missing, unsuitable, or unclaimed, or if a helper cancels or cannot complete a task, the organization records the exception and decides the next action.

A first pilot should use the partner’s existing collection point, facilities, and volunteer network where possible. It should begin with support categories that partner already knows how to handle. Specialized or sensitive requests can be referred to qualified organizations rather than handled without suitable procedures.

## Measuring value and overhead

Helping Hands should measure both beneficiary outcomes and the effort required to deliver them. Useful indicators include:

- time from request submission to review;
- time from approval to fulfillment;
- share of approved requests completed or partially completed;
- requests waiting for a suitable resource;
- coordinator hours per completed request;
- volunteer hours committed and completed;
- goods received, reserved, delivered, or written off;
- transport, storage, facility, and direct spending costs;
- cancellations, failed deliveries, complaints, and reassignments.

These measures show whether the organization can fulfill requests with the resources it has, what creates delays, and where added support would help.

## A manageable first version

The business model can describe many needs and contributors. The first course prototype can demonstrate a few complete paths:

1. A beneficiary requests goods and a donor offer is received, checked, reserved, and delivered.
2. A third party submits a request and an authorized coordinator reviews and prioritizes it.
3. A partner organization requests help, and a volunteer offers tutoring, repairs, or another skill.
4. A coordinator matches a transport or facility offer to an approved request.
5. A money contribution is recorded as received and allocated by the partner organization, without processing actual payments in the prototype.
6. Each path records coordinator effort, volunteer hours, resource use, status changes, and completion evidence.

Use fictional people and sample data for the demonstration. This scope gives the project meaningful business rules, multiple user roles, goods tracking, scheduling, accountability, and overhead reporting.

For a real pilot in Egypt, financial collection and organizational operation need to be checked with an appropriate local partner. Law No. 149 of 2019 and its implementing regulations include requirements about donations and fundraising; the specific obligations depend on the operator and collection method.
